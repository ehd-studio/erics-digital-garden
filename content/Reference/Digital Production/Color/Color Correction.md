Color correction is an objective, technical process designed to normalize, balance, and restore visual data to a state of naturalistic accuracy. By adjusting elements such as exposure, white balance, contrast, and saturation, color correction fixes technical flaws and lighting issues so that an image or video accurately represents the real-world scene as the human eye would perceive it.
While the term is most heavily associated with digital media (photography and video editing), its underlying scientific principles are widely applied across multiple industries, including print, cosmetology, and makeup artistry.
### Color Correction in Photography and Video
In digital post-production, color correction is considered a foundational "clean-up phase" that occurs before any stylistic choices are made. It is distinctly different from [[Color Grading]], which is a subjective process used to create a specific artistic mood or cinematic style. Why it is essential:
- **Shot-to-Shot Consistency:** In video production, filming conditions and camera settings often fluctuate. Color correction ensures that brightness and colors remain cohesive from clip to clip, preventing jarring transitions that could distract the viewer.
- **The Foundation for Grading:** Applying a creative color grade to uncorrected footage is likened to "painting on a dirty canvas". A neutral, corrected baseline is required so that subsequent stylistic adjustments react consistently across a scene.
- **Brand and E-commerce Accuracy:** For commercial photography and retail, color correction ensures that product images look true to life, which is critical for maintaining brand identity, meeting customer expectations, and reducing product return rates.
### Technical Tools
Because the human eye can be easily fooled by room lighting and uncalibrated screens, professionals rely on calibrated reference monitors and mathematical video scopes to achieve true accuracy.
- **Waveform Monitors** are used to evaluate luminance (brightness), ensuring that details are not lost to "crushed" blacks or "clipped" whites.
- **Vectorscopes** display chrominance (color hue and saturation) and are highly relied upon to ensure that skin tones sit accurately on the designated "flesh line," regardless of the subject's ethnicity.
- **RGB Parades** separate the red, green, and blue channels to easily identify and neutralize unwanted color casts.

## Color Correction vs. [[Color Grading]]
The main difference between color correction and color grading comes down to their intent: color correction restores an image to how it should accurately look in reality, while color grading transforms it into how the artist wants it to look and feel.
While both processes alter the colors in your footage and happen in post-production, they are distinct steps with different goals:
- **Mindset (Objective vs. Creative):** 
	- *Correction*: technical, objective process focused on fixing errors. It involves adjusting exposure, white balance, and contrast to ensure the footage looks natural, accurate, and true to the real world. 
	- *Grading*: highly subjective and creative process. It deliberately manipulates colors to evoke emotions, establish a visual aesthetic, and support storytelling—such as using warm, golden tones for nostalgia, or cool, blue tones for suspense.
- **Order of Operations (Prep vs. Polish):** 
	- *Correction*: foundational first step. It cleans up the image and provides a neutral baseline. Color grading is the polish that happens afterward. 
	- *Grading*: applying a creative color grade to uncorrected footage is highly discouraged, as it acts like painting on a dirty canvas and will only amplify underlying technical flaws.
- **Workflow Scale (Clip-by-clip vs. Global):** 
	- *Correction*: typically applied on a shot-by-shot basis to balance individual clips, ensuring that footage shot with different cameras or in shifting lighting conditions matches seamlessly. 
	- *Grading*: often operates on a broader scale, applying a unified aesthetic or stylistic filter over an entire scene or sequence to maintain a cohesive look.
- **Visibility (Unnoticed vs. Signature):** 
	- *Correction*: should go completely unnoticed by the audience, providing a smooth viewing experience without distracting inconsistencies. 
	- *Grading*: meant to be seen and felt, often becoming a signature look or recognizable visual identity for a filmmaker or brand.

## Process
The workflow generally begins with primary correction to balance the overall image, followed by secondary correction, where tools like masks or qualifiers are used to isolate and fix specific problem areas, such as an overly vibrant t-shirt or an underexposed background window.
#### Setup and Normalization 
The process begins by importing and organizing the footage, followed by [[Color Management]]. This step often involves mathematically transforming flat "log" footage from the camera into a standard display color space ([[Color Gamut]]), such as Rec 709, so that it can be accurately evaluated.
#### Primary Correction (Neutralizing and Matching)
The first active editing step involves balancing the overall exposure, white balance, and contrast to ensure the image looks natural to the human eye. Editors use visual scopes, like histograms, waveforms, and vectorscopes, to check that black levels are truly black, white points are pure white, and primary RGB colors are balanced without unwanted color casts. A crucial part of this stage is shot matching, which ensures that brightness and color remain cohesive from clip to clip across an entire scene.
#### Exposure Fixes 
After the global balance is set, the colorist addresses isolated luminance issues, such as recovering detail in a blown-out background window or lifting shadows on a subject's dark shirt. This is typically achieved using power windows or masks, which allow the editor to isolate and adjust specific areas without affecting the overall image exposure.
#### Secondary Color Fixes: 
This step targets specific problem colors that were not resolved in the primary pass. Technicians use tools like qualifiers, tracking, or HSL (Hue, Saturation, Lightness) controls to fix issues like an overly vibrant t-shirt or to ensure that skin tones look natural.

By completing these technical fixes first, the footage acts as a perfect "blank canvas" for the subsequent, creative color grading phase.
