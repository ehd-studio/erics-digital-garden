Color grading is the creative and artistic phase of video and film post-production where colors are manipulated to establish a specific mood, atmosphere, or visual style.

Unlike color correction—which is an objective, technical process used to fix errors and create a neutral, true-to-life baseline—color grading is highly subjective. It involves deliberately altering the image to support the story, turning a corrected "blank canvas" into a finalized cinematic piece.

---
### The Role of Color in Storytelling

Color grading is fundamentally rooted in color theory and human psychology. By shifting hues, saturation, and contrast, colorists can influence how an audience feels during a scene:
- **Setting the Mood:** Warm tones (reds, oranges, yellows) can evoke feelings of passion, energy, or nostalgia, while cool tones (blues, greens) often suggest isolation, tension, or sadness.
- **Guiding the Audience:** Grading can direct the viewer's eye to specific details, such as brightening a character's face or using a discordant pop of color to highlight a crucial object (like the red coat in _Schindler's List_).
- **Common Cinematic Styles:** Certain palettes have become staples in filmmaking. The **"Teal and Orange"** look is heavily used in blockbusters to make warm skin tones stand out against cool, blue-green shadows. Other popular looks include the desaturated **Bleach Bypass** for gritty, war-like realism, or warm **Sepia** for vintage nostalgia.

---
### The Evolution of Color Grading

Color manipulation has evolved dramatically alongside filmmaking technology:

- **Photochemical Color Timing:** Before digital editing, grading was an analog laboratory process called "color timing." Technicians working in darkrooms adjusted red, green, and blue "printer lights" to alter the exposure of the positive print from the camera negative.
- **The Digital Intermediate (DI):** The major turning point came in the late 1990s and early 2000s when films began being scanned into high-resolution digital files, graded on computers, and printed back to film. _O Brother, Where Art Thou?_ (2000) is famously recognized as the first Hollywood feature to undergo a complete DI process, allowing the filmmakers to digitally shift lush green foliage into a dusty, dry brown to achieve a specific aesthetic.
- **Modern Digital Mastery:** Today, color grading is fully digital, utilizing high-end software that allows real-time, non-destructive editing and granular control over individual pixels.

---
### Workflow
To maintain image quality, professional colorists follow a strict "order of operations":
1. **Normalization & Correction:** Logarithmic (Log) footage—which looks flat and gray straight out of the camera to preserve dynamic range—is first converted to a standard viewing [[Color Gamut]]. The shot is then color-corrected to fix exposure and white balance, and matched to surrounding shots.
2. **Look Development:** The creative style is applied to the balanced footage. This is often done using Look-Up Tables (LUTs), which are pre-defined mathematical color presets that can instantly apply a complex, cinematic transformation or emulate classic analog film stocks.
3. **Secondary Adjustments:** The colorist isolates specific areas to refine. They use **Power Windows** (geometric masks) to brighten or darken specific parts of the frame, and **Qualifiers** to select and alter specific colors, like smoothing out a subject's skin tone.

---
### Software and Equipment
The tools used for color grading dictate how the colorist thinks and works:
- **Software Architectures:** Programs like **DaVinci Resolve** use a _node-based_ architecture, allowing colorists to link individual adjustments in a complex, flowchart-like web. Conversely, software like **Adobe Premiere Pro** (via the Lumetri panel) or **FilmLight Baselight** use a _layer-based_ approach, stacking adjustments vertically like in Photoshop.
- **The Environment:** Because human eyes easily adapt to surrounding light, grading must be done in a highly controlled environment. Professional suites feature neutral gray walls, zero outside sunlight, and D65 (6500K) bias lighting placed behind the monitor to prevent eye strain and ensure accurate color perception.
- **Scopes:** Colorists rely on mathematical graphs rather than their eyes to judge images. Tools like the **Waveform Monitor** (for brightness) and the **Vectorscope** (for hue and saturation) ensure the final image is technically flawless and legally safe for broadcast.
