### Title
This creative brief documents the organizational structure for Mission Community Woodshop's (MCW) integrated media strategy for attracting more donors and artists to their programs.
## Organizational Background
Mission Community Woodshop (MCW) is a nonprofit, sliding scale woodshop in San Francisco that offers classes, memberships, and group experiences for schools, community organizations, and businesses, each designed around the partner’s goals. Its founders, Andrew Salverda and Dan Matz, are long-time friends and teachers whose members range from professionals and amateurs to surfers, poets, tech entrepreneurs, and professors. The sliding scale is central to everything MCW does: students and members pay what they can, and anyone for whom even the lowest price is a stretch is invited to ask for additional discounts. MCW has not turned anyone away to date, and because its programs cost money, it relies heavily on donations from its wider community to keep them accessible.
MCW’s goal is a tight-knit, inclusive community of woodworkers, from accomplished to amateur, that is part of something larger than any one person. Making woodworking accessible to a wide range of San Franciscans is a core commitment: MCW helps newcomers get comfortable on its machines, guides them through their first projects, and offers flexible tuition and bench rental fees as its finances allow. The founders are launching this endeavor now and welcome drop-ins, tours, bench rentals, classes, and volunteers.
The shop includes a long list of courses and amenities for shop members. It also offers a fellowship program supporting emerging furniture makers and aspiring woodworking teachers who get access to professional-grade machines, a workbench, and mentorship in its Mission District shop while teaching about nine hours a week and building their own furniture and shop skills. Fellows are expected to spend about thirty hours a week in the shop, may receive a stipend of up to $3,000 per month based on demonstrated need, and close the program with a public display of their completed pieces.

## Campaign Objective
Launch MCW communication across all social and web platforms under a unified brand identity in order to communicate more strongly with prospective donors and support stronger outreach to interested parties.

### Insights and Opportunities
- **Making woodworking accessible to a wide range of San Franciscans.** The sliding scale, flexible tuition, and bench rental options let MCW reach people who would be priced out of most Bay Area craft programs. Its record of not turning anyone away is a credible story for media and donors.
- **Offering professional-grade equipment at a community price.** The shop’s vintage and professional machines, including Yates bandsaws, Powermatic jointers and planers, and SawStop table saws, are rare in a community setting. Many hobbyists and students will never get access to equipment like this, so MCW can position itself as a place to learn on machines usually found only in professional or institutional shops.
- **Serving as a bridge for schools and mid-career creatives.** The group experiences let MCW design programs around a partner’s goals, while the fellowship develops emerging furniture makers and teachers. Together they give MCW a pipeline of teachers, partners, and makers that can strengthen both its programs and its reputation.
- **Building a community around the shop’s Mission District location.** The 5,250-square-foot space near Valencia and Duboce, with a classroom, machine room, and member bench room, lets MCW anchor a local craft community. Drop-ins, tours, and public showcases can make the shop a neighborhood destination, not just a class venue.
- **Turning the shop’s history and craftsmanship into a shared story.** Many of the machines date from the 1930s to the 1990s, and the founders and members come from varied backgrounds, from teachers and tech entrepreneurs to surfers and poets. That mix gives MCW content with real character, which supports the community-focused social strategy and helps it stand out from more generic maker spaces.
### Success Metrics
- **Class and membership sign-ups:** This is the most direct measure of new members and paid participation, which are central to MCW’s earned revenue.
- **Click-to-booking conversion:** This shows whether the website turns interested visitors into bookings, so it reveals whether the digital funnel works once people arrive.
- **Tour and drop-in visits:** These show whether online interest converts into in-person engagement, the step that most often leads to memberships and classes.
- **Audience involvement:** Sustained comments, likes, shares, and follows indicate whether MCW’s content keeps people connected to the brand long enough to visit, which makes it the main leading indicator for the other metrics.
- **First-time and repeat donations:** Since MCW relies heavily on community support to fund its sliding scale, new donors and their retention show whether the campaign is generating the money that keeps programs accessible.

## Positioning
### Message/Promise
MCW believes woodworking should be open to anyone in San Francisco, regardless of budget or experience. Its sliding scale, professional-grade tools, and supportive mentors let people learn a craft and build something lasting. The result is an inclusive, tight-knit community that is bigger than any one person.
### Support
- **The sliding scale is central to how MCW operates.** The about page says it has not turned anyone away to date, and students who find even the lowest price a stretch are invited to ask for additional discounts. Flexible tuition and bench rental fees, and a nonprofit model supported by donations, make the promise real rather than aspirational.
- **The founders are teachers.** Andrew Salverda and Dan Matz have taught at Urban School, SF Friends, Randall Museum, and Nueva, so the instruction behind the promise comes from people with long experience teaching.
- **The shop has professional-grade equipment.** The facilities page lists machines such as Yates bandsaws, Martin planers and jointers, SawStop table saws, and a Felder slot mortiser, and the class list shows that beginners use them. That is what makes “open to anyone” a substantive offer rather than a generic one.
- **The class structure supports newcomers.** Classes like Cutting Boards and Spoon Carving are short and affordable entry points, while longer projects like the Ladderback Chair and Dovetail Bench build skills step by step. Open Shop is available to students who have completed prerequisite classes.
- **The community is visible in the programs.** Several classes were designed by members, such as the Keepsake Box and Three-legged Stool, and the members range from professionals and amateurs to surfers, poets, tech entrepreneurs, and professors. The fellowship, which supports emerging furniture makers and teachers, extends that community into the next generation of makers.

## Risks/Constraints
#### Privacy
- **Consent and privacy:** Members, students, and fellows must agree to appear on camera, and that consent should be specific about where content will be posted and for how long. Students in school group experiences are often minors, so MCW needs parental or school permission and should avoid identifying them by name or school without approval.
- **Donor and financial privacy:** Stories about the sliding scale or stipends can reveal what individuals pay or receive. MCW should not show individual pricing or income-based details without consent, and it should describe the model in aggregate.
- **Neighborhood and studio considerations:** Filming in a shared Mission District building may raise concerns about noise, lighting, other tenants’ privacy, and the appearance of the space. Shoots should be scheduled around classes and members’ work, and other people in the shop should give consent before appearing.
#### Shop Safety
- **Safety and liability:** Videos of machines in use can normalize risky practices or be copied by viewers who lack training. MCW should show guards and safety equipment, avoid filming unsafe operations, and make sure the content matches what its waivers and instruction cover.
#### Cost
- **Capacity and production cost:** Video production takes time from instructors and staff, who are already running classes and a fellowship. A small team may struggle to keep a consistent posting schedule, so MCW should plan a realistic cadence and batch-film where possible.
#### Platform
- **Platform volatility:** Algorithm changes, shifting audience preferences, and platform policy changes can reduce reach overnight. MCW should keep an owned channel, such as its website or email list, as a foundation for conversions that do not depend on any one platform.
#### Brand
- **Brand consistency across contributors:** The community includes many members with different voices, so interviews and member-made content can drift from MCW’s warm, playful, craft-focused tone. Style guidance and a simple review process help keep the message consistent.
- **Accessibility:** Captions, alt text, and clear audio make content usable for deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers and for people watching without sound, which matters for a community organization.
- **Authenticity and overpromising:** Content that shows polished finished pieces without the learning process can make beginners feel they will fall short. Showing real progress, including mistakes and the time involved, supports MCW’s inviting message.

## Brand Guidelines
### Tone of Voice
MCW’s brand voice is warm, welcoming, and playful, with a confident, craft-focused edge. The about page speaks directly to the reader (“Please join us!” and “We look forward to seeing you”), and it describes the community in friendly, human terms, such as “good-natured woodworkers” and a mix of “surfers and poets, tech entrepreneurs and professors.” The class descriptions add personality with lines like “A fun, satisfying project!” and “Master these, and the world is your oyster,” while still taking the craft seriously by highlighting professional-grade machines and skill-building. The voice is inclusive and humble about the shop’s history (“We’ve built the woodworking space of our (and hopefully your) dreams”) but never condescending to beginners. Overall, MCW sounds like a knowledgeable, generous friend who wants you in the shop, not a formal institution.

### Approval Protocol
Andrew Salverda and Dan Matz approve content before it goes public. The process below is designed to keep turnaround fast while protecting consent, privacy, and brand voice.
1. **Content creation.** The media lead or a designated member drafts the post, caption, or video and attaches the signed release forms for everyone shown.
2. **Compliance check.** The media lead confirms that consent is on file, that no minors or donors are identified without permission, that no unlicensed music or images are used, and that safety equipment is visible where machines are shown.
3. **Brand review.** A second staff member checks tone, accessibility (captions and alt text), and accuracy of any class details, prices, or dates against the current class list and website.
4. **Founder approval.** Andrew or Dan reviews the item in a shared approval folder or tool. Either founder can approve routine posts. Both approve any content involving donors, schools, fellows, stipends, or sliding-scale details.
5. **Scheduling.** Once approved, the post is scheduled with the approval noted in the content calendar.
6. **Post-publication log.** The media lead records the post date, channel, UTM link, and performance metrics in the tracking dashboard.
**Approval turnaround and escalation**
- Routine posts: approval within 2 business days.
- Time-sensitive posts, such as same-week class openings: approval within 24 hours, with a founder reachable by phone.
- If a founder does not respond within the set window, the item is held rather than published. It is never approved by default.
- Disagreements between the founders are resolved by discussion. If they cannot agree, the content is not published until they do.
**Review cadence**
- Weekly 30-minute check-in on the content calendar and pending approvals.
- Monthly review of performance data with the founders, using the five priority metrics.
- Quarterly review of consent records, takedown requests, and any feedback from members or partners.
