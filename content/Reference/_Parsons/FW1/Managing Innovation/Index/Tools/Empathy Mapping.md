An **empathy map** is a collaborative visualization tool used to organize, articulate, and synthesize what we know about a particular type of user. Originally created by **Dave Gray and his team at XPLANE** in the early 2000s, it serves to externalize user insights, align cross-functional teams around a shared vocabulary, and guide human-centered design decisions during product or service discovery.

While an **audience persona** defines _who_ the user is by summarizing their long-term demographics, goals, and behavioral patterns, an empathy map captures _what_ they are experiencing in a **particular moment or specific scenario**.

---

### The Core Four Quadrants

In its traditional format, an empathy map is split into **four quadrants** arranged around a central user or persona:

1. **Says:** This section contains **what the user says out loud** during interviews, user-testing sessions, or field studies. Ideally, it is populated with direct, verbatim quotes. Capturing their exact phrasing helps keep the team grounded in concrete evidence rather than speculative paraphrasing.
2. **Thinks:** This quadrant captures **what occupies the user's thoughts** throughout their experience, especially what they might be reluctant to vocalize. These insights are inferred from reading between the lines, noting hesitations, or examining context.
3. **Does:** This section documents the **observable physical actions, behaviors, and workarounds** the user takes during task completion (e.g., _refreshes page several times_ or _writes notes on paper before entering data_). It highlights the gap between what users say they do and what they actually do.
4. **Feels:** This captures the **emotional state of the user**, usually represented by an adjective and a short sentence for context (e.g., _confused: too many contradictory prices_ or _anxious: worried they are doing something wrong_). This quadrant helps teams look beyond functional complaints toward understanding the emotional experience underneath.

---

### The Extended Sections (Modern Frameworks)

As empathy mapping evolved, practitioners like the **Nielsen Norman Group** and healthcare entities began adapting the canvas. Many modern templates feature additional sections to capture sensory inputs and structural goals:

- **Hear:** What the user reads, or what they hear from friends, family, colleagues, and influential authority figures that impacts their worldview.
- **See:** What the user physically sees in their immediate environment, organizational dynamics, or broader market trends while executing a task.
- **Pains (Challenges):** The core frustrations, obstacles, fears, or red tape blocking the user from successfully reaching their goal.
- **Gains (Opportunities):** The user's ultimate hopes, dreams, aspirations, or the positive outcomes they expect to experience once their friction is resolved.

---

### The Real Power: Finding Tensions and Gaps

The true value of an empathy map does not come from filling out the quadrants in isolation; it comes from **identifying contradictions and tensions across the quadrants**.

When a team analyzes all sections together, they often spot revealing inconsistencies—such as a user who _says_ "I want more features" but _does_ a workaround because they are _feeling_ overwhelmed by the current UI. These frictions and misalignments are where the most valuable design insights and opportunities for innovation live.
