The **Hook Framework** (commonly known as the **Hooked Model**) is a four-phase behavioral design framework created by Stanford lecturer and author Nir Eyal in his 2014 book, _Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products_.

The primary objective of the model is to boost user retention by guiding users through a repeating loop of **Trigger, Action, Variable Reward, and Investment**. By cycling through these stages repeatedly, opening and using a product stops being a conscious decision and instead becomes an automatic, self-initiated habit or "reflex".

Product teams rely on this behavioral psychology because user retention is incredibly challenging—global benchmarks show that typical apps lose 70% to 80% of their users within 30 days of install, and establishing a robust habit loop within the first 14 days is critical to surviving this "retention cliff".

---

### The Four Phases of the Hook Loop

#### 1. The Trigger

The trigger is the spark plug or cue that prompts the behavior. Triggers are divided into two types:

- **External Triggers:** These carry explicit information about what the user should do next (e.g., a push notification, an email, or an app icon).
- **Internal Triggers:** These are emotions, routines, or situations that cue the product automatically from memory. Boredom might trigger a social feed, loneliness a messaging app, or uncertainty a search engine. _The ultimate goal of a habit-forming product is to transition users from relying on external triggers to responding to internal ones_.

#### 2. The Action

The action is the simplest physical behavior done in anticipation of a reward (e.g., opening an app, clicking "play," or scrolling a feed). Drawing from **BJ Fogg's Behavior Model (B=MAP)**, a behavior only occurs when **Motivation**, **Ability**, and a **Prompt** converge at the same moment. Therefore, the action phase must require minimal cognitive load and friction; if the action is too difficult, the loop breaks.

#### 3. The Variable Reward

This is the neurological engine of the loop. While predictable rewards eventually lose their appeal and engagement fades, **variable (unpredictable) rewards** spike dopamine and keep users hooked. Nir Eyal categorizes these into three types:

- **Rewards of the Tribe:** Social validation and connection (e.g., likes, comments, and social feeds).
- **Rewards of the Hunt:** The search for information, resources, or "loot" (e.g., scrolling to find a good video or item).
- **Rewards of the Self:** Personal gratification through mastery, completion, and achievements (e.g., clearing an inbox, completing a game level, or maintaining a streak).

#### 4. The Investment

This is the most frequently neglected stage of behavioral design. During this phase, the user puts something back into the product—such as data, personal preferences, custom playlists, followers, or effort. This investment serves two critical purposes:

- It utilizes the **"IKEA effect"**, where users value the product more because they have invested work into it, raising their switching costs.
- It **loads the next trigger**. For example, sending a message (investment) prompts a reply, which generates a push notification (external trigger) that restarts the loop.

### Real-World Examples of the Hook Model in Use

#### 1. Duolingo (Language Learning)

- **Trigger:** Starts with a daily push notification (external). Over time, this graduates into the internal emotional trigger of wanting to protect progress.
- **Action:** Completing a quick, gamified, low-friction language lesson that takes only a couple of minutes.
- **Variable Reward:** Earning points, surprise level-ups, mystery chests, and visual progress indicators (rewards of the _self_).
- **Investment:** Accumulating XP and building a **Daily Streak**. The streak itself is a stored-value investment—users develop psychological ownership over it and refuse to lose it, prompting them to log in again tomorrow.

#### 2. Calm (Mental Wellness App)

- **Trigger:** A personalized push notification or a scheduled time for daily meditation.
- **Action:** Tapping to start a breathing exercise or guided meditation.
- **Variable Reward:** Intrinsic feelings of calm, stress relief, or tracking emotional patterns.
- **Investment:** Setting personalized notification triggers and logging daily streaks. Keeping the streak alive acts as a psychological sunk cost, retaining millions of paid subscribers.

#### 3. Instagram (Social Media)

- **Trigger:** Boredom or a feeling of FOMO (internal triggers).
- **Action:** Tapping the app icon and scrolling down the feed.
- **Variable Reward:** An unpredictable mix of beautiful photos (rewards of the _hunt_) and likes/comments on one's own photos (rewards of the _tribe_).
- **Investment:** Following new accounts, posting photos, and writing captions, which in turn cues notifications for others, restarting the cycle.

#### 4. Banx (Sustainability-Focused Fintech)

- **Trigger:** Making a routine purchase or checking a bank balance (functional triggers).
- **Action:** Opening the banking app to review transaction logs.
- **Variable Reward:** Seeing real-time CO2 savings calculated dynamically based on where they spent money, converting static banking into an emotionally rewarding environmental impact tracking loop.
- **Investment:** Aligning their financial habits with their personal values and building a cumulative "green" financial profile.

### The Ethical Boundary: Habits vs. Addictions

Because the Hook Model is highly effective at altering human behavior, it raises major ethical questions. Eyal stresses that the framework should be used to build healthy habits rather than destructive addictions. He proposes the **Manipulation Matrix** as an ethical check: designers should ask themselves:

1. _Does this product materially improve the user's life?_
2. _Would I use this product myself?_

When organizations apply habit loops purely to exploit users for "engagement at all costs" (such as ad-supported doomscrolling or utilizing dark patterns to trap users), they cross into manipulation. Ethical, sustainable habit design focuses on delivering **genuine, undeniable value** that solves a real human problem so well that the user naturally wants to return.
