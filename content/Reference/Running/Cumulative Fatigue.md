Expected accumulation of fatigue over days, weeks or months of consistent, long-distance training.
## Components of Fatigue
Building this 'positive fatigue' rather than overworking can be attributed to a couple of different, but connected, concepts.
### Mileage
Mileage is the foundation everything else is built upon. Regardless of your distance goals, you will need to create a schedule that prepares your body for the mileage you want to run. If you run a 5k at a steady pace, you will most likely need to find how to run 4-6 times that distance within a week eventually. Its recommended that marathon runners complete 2-3 times their distance of 26 miles (roughly 50-70 miles) in order to prepare their body for the fatigue they'll face. While not required out the gate, its recommended to give your body and training schedule the time to ladder yourself up to the distance described. 
### Intensity
Pace for your miles is key to the physiological development as a runner. Adaptations of endurance training, like muscle fiber changes and ability to burn fat, are all available through controlling slower than anaerobic threshold paces. Similarly, strength workouts and tempo runs help to maximize your anaerobic threshold and increase your speed safely overtime. If you're able to keep your pace in check, you set yourself up to tolerate higher training volumes and receive the benefits of endurance training. If you decide to forgo pacing, you run the risk of not recovering sufficiently and changing cumulative fatigue into overwork. 
### Balance
The long run at the end of your week will not make your marathon. Similarly, easy runs will not give you the physiological benefits needed for longer endurance requirements. And with too much of either, you'll most likely get bored. Balancing easy runs with your workouts (strength, tempo, speed and long runs) gives variability that not only helps you reap the physiological benefits, but can keep things interesting over the weeks or months you plan to train. 
### Consistency
Fitness gains rely on consistent training over time. Proper training can help a runner gain rapid improvement through proper training, but that progress will be lost with just a week or two of inconsistency. The key is *something is better than nothing*. While life is unpredictable and hard at times, create a baseline needing to be achieved and find ways to put in work anyway you can.
For creating proper goals I have two main suggestions:
- Create attainable, exciting goals. They help push you out the door when its the last thing you want to do. If you set your sights too high, you'll likely get discouraged. If you set them too low, you'll get board. Find the right amount of push to get your drive moving
- Write out your run plan for the week or month ahead. Its easier to adjust when you can see how your workouts align with your goal. Writing it out in advance makes you think about your week ahead while also creating blocks that can be adjusted if things come up.
### Recovery
In order to preserve physiological adaptations, recovery does not include prolonged periods of lounging. Instead, cumulative fatigue helps you mitigate the tougher workouts with easier runs that burn fat instead of carbs (glycogen) and can keep your activity up while still building muscle. The fatigue is normal and partial recovery is expected. If you are to train your body to withstand extended mileage, you want to help it to adapt to the last 16 miles rather than the first. Get really good with nutrition, stretching, and active recovery. Additional acts like contrast immersion or ice baths can also help with muscle fatigue.
