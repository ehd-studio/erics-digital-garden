#### History
Sashiko emerged during the Edo period (1603-1867) as a way for Farmers and Fishermen to mend clothing and reinforce garments to add warmth during the cold Northern Japanese winters as well as duck sumptuary laws. Cotton and other trade fabrics were embargoed by the Tokugawa shogunate so the only way for most to get a hold of it was through tiny rags of fabric from southern tradesmen ([source](https://www.bbc.com/culture/article/20231013-the-300-year-old-japanese-method-of-upcyling)). If one could find a piece, they would connect those pieces to their clothing through a series of stitches. The resulting clothing was named *boro boro* (meaning 'tattered clothing or rags'). The technique was typically done on Indigo clothing (as it was Japanese indigo plant was easy to grow) with white or red hemp threads.
The practice began to fade during Japans industrialization stage as the mending was no longer necessary as a daily chore. However, western quilting gained popularity in the 1970s bolstering a resurgence of the technique. 
#### Ainu Embroidery
Though not Sashiko, the Ainu tribe of Northern Japan also had a similar stitch technique called *[[Kaparamip]]*(thin garment). Their technique focused on spiritual protection motifs mended in spiral and thorn designs. It was primarily utilized to imbue ceremonial robes and women for spiritual support. They used high-density chain stitches versus other regional running stitches for clothing reinforcement.

Over time, running stitches came to hold more symbolism and craftsmanship. Sashiko artisans added symbolism to their designs in an effort to embue the clothing with positive intentions to the recipient. Examples of this practice:
>麻の葉 (Asa no ha) - Hemp leaf design. Hemp grows straight and quick mirroring the symbolism of health, vigor and resilience. Also a lucky omen pattern.

Designs are regional and their is an extensive library of designs that can be accessed throughout the country. 

#### Needle
Sashiko needles are typically longer to help with the running stitch (multiple threads at a time). The ideal stitch size is no bigger than a kernel of short grain rice.
#### Thread
Sashiko thread was devised to be able to break through three layers of fabric without fraying. 4-6 strands of 100% cotton thread are twisted tightly into a single strand to create a non-divisble line. This differs from the divisibility of embroidery floss. The thread is also not mercerized (sheen with chemicals) adding to its ability to blend with fabric and grip to multiple layers of fabric. The matte finish allows it to also be stored in thread braids. Thickness is not uniform on thread so its important to understand brands of thickness.
