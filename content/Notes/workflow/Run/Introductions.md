## Foundations
A lot of the methods discussed later have foundations within the history of how modern long distance running has been popularized. Training plans like the Hanson Method and Lydiard Training Method have been integrated into more modern training regiments, but have foundations in the past century of running. With that being said, a very short intro to the history of recreational running is needed.
Recreational jogging experienced a 'second boom' in popularity in the 1970s bolstered by a series of events including:
- Frank Shorter's victory in the men's marathon at the 1972 Summer Olympics
- Increased emphasis on Health and Wellness lifestyles
- Title IX mandating gender equality in scholastic athletic events
- Increased broadcasts of marathons like Boston, New York, Bay to Breakers and Chicago.
- Jim Fixx's 'The Complete Book of Running'
- 'Rocky' training montage running the streets of Philadelphia
The popularity led to a massive increase not only in marathon competitors, but everyday joggers. Surges led to popularization of new running shoes like Blue Ribbon's Waffle Racer (soon to be Nike) and training plans from legend like Bill Bowerman and Arthur Lydiard. This foundation laid the groundwork for our modern landscape of how we view training and athletic performance in running. If you're interested in the background I'd recommend a couple of books:
- *Shoe Dog* by Phil Knight - rise of Nike during the outset of the 1970s running craze
- *Boston Marathon: The History of the World's Premier Running Event* by Tom Derderian - history of the race and background on how training changed over the years
- *The Perfect Mile* by Neal Bascomb - breaking the 4 minute mile time
- *Speed with Endurance* by Bill Squires - older book deep diving running training and its creation
- *ChiRunning* by Danny Dreyer

## Philosophy
Anyways, how does this connect with your training? Understanding how to create a plan that tailors to your schedule and needs requires an understanding of the foundations for planning. The 1970s explosion of marathon jogging created a lifestyle that relied on what famous NZ running coach Arthur Lydiard called [[Cumulative Fatigue]]. That is repetitive training that doesn't allow for a runner to fully recover between training sessions. The more times you train per week, the less time your body has to recover between training sessions. The key to you creating a plan that works and not injuring yourself is to understand the difference between cumulative fatigue and overworking:
- [[Cumulative Fatigue]] - expected **tiredness** and **soreness** across a training block, but generally still more energy left. Performance is not hindered and strength allows for increased training loads.
- *Overworking/Overtraining* - **pain** and **stress** during routine runs. Recovery time between hard runs is incomplete and you struggle to keep consistent pace across your runs. You may feel like you're getting weaker. It may also affect how well you sleep and a loss of appetite.
Understanding the difference is key to not putting yourself in danger of injury or falling off a plan. 
## Physiology
NOTE: I am by no means a Physiologist. This is a *very* basic overview of what parts of the body interact with marathon training
### Marathon Muscles
As we move and expel energy, we use what are called skeletal muscles. These muscles consist of cable like structures of Muscle Fibers that are controlled by motor neurons. Each bundle of muscle fibers contains one of two types: slow-twitch and fast-twitch (of which there are three sub categories). Each bundle contains one muscle fiber, thousands of bundles create a muscle and each bundle is connected to a motor neuron controlled by the central nervous system. 
In endurance sports like running we are primarily concerned with Type 1 Fibers or [[Slow Twitch Fibers]]

## Brands
Look good, feel good! Its time to start looking at [[Equipment]]. You know how to shop most likely, but I'm here to give a couple ideas based on my own experience shopping for running equipment. 
- *Talk with someone about shoes* - The single most important purchase is the shoe. Luckily there are an abundance of options. When beginning training, you may realize what your shoes lack or what they do well. With that knowledge and your training routine, go talk with someone and try on a bunch of shoes. 
- *Think about purchasing two pairs* - With the repetitive motion of running and the volume you'll be doing it over your training regiment, your shoes will take a beating. Sneakers absorb somewhere between 1.5 and 2 times your body weight every time your foot hits the ground. With that force, the foam on your shoes needs roughly 24-48 hours to decompress before running again. A second pair of running shoes provides an option for those off hours and allows the other pair time to decompress between runs.
- *Nothing beats a good pair of socks* - Day or night run you'll always need socks. There are a lot of investments to make for run gear, but great socks is a great start and you won't regret getting a couple of good pairs.
